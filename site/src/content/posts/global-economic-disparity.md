Today’s world can be called a world where economic fundamentalism rules which implies supremacy of economics in all the affairs of the world. With the fall of Soviet Union, economic fundamentalism has taken the form of Corporate Fundamentalism where Corporate World has become the virtual leader of the world. All the governmental, non-government, national as well as international institutions are directly or indirectly under its control. The growth models adopted by countries including India are largely aimed at realizing the interests of the Corporate. The result is that the wealth produced is being distributed in a way that economic disparity is multiplying much faster than the real growth. The truth is that the “growth” today in effect means nothing but the growth of the market, without any consideration to its impact on the masses, which are more negative than positive. Absolute poverty may be decreasing but the economic disparity is increasing the relative poverty in the country. Obviously the communities and sections of society that are poor are the biggest sufferers. Under these circumstances, a decrease in the absolute poverty level has hardly any significance.

It is high time the role of Big Business and the institutions created by the modern economic order were re-examined so that the adverse effects of the current economic model could be reversed. But before discussing the implications and solutions, let us understand what Economic Disparity means, how it is measured, and what the levels of global and Indian disparity are.

According to popular definition, Economic inequality or Disparity is an indicator of the gap between the rich and the poor and comprises disparities in the distribution of [economic](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic "Economic") [assets](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assets "Assets") (wealth) and [income](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Income "Income") within individuals of a country or region, between regions or between various groups based on religion, caste, class or culture. The term typically refers to inequality among individuals and groups within a society, but can also refer to [inequality among countries](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_inequality "International inequality"). **While an absolute economic equality is impossible, the extreme forms of disparity are dangerous for society, and need to be addressed for the sake of the larger population.** The issue of economic inequality involves various issues including the ideas of [equity](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equity_%28economics%29 "Equity (economics)"), [equality of outcome](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equality_of_outcome "Equality of outcome"), [equality of opportunity](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equality_of_opportunity "Equality of opportunity"), the causes and factors leading to the widening disparity and the solutions. Certain forces of economics have of course been supporting the idea of economic inequality on various grounds, most of which are aimed at perpetuating their hold on economy and other power centres.

Observers differ on both the morality and utility of inequality, whether, and/or how much inequality is necessary in society and how it can be affected. It has been praised as necessary and beneficial, mainly by the proponents of capitalistic systems, and attacked as a growing social problem, mainly by the sociologists but also by the followers of different religions and other ideologies.

**Disparity may broadly be divided into Income Disparity and Wealth Disparity. Obviously, if the Income Disparity continues, in absence of any corrective measures, the Wealth Disparity will grow bigger and bigger.**

Differences in national income equality around the world are measured by the national [Gini coefficient](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gini_coefficient "Gini coefficient"). The Gini coefficient is a number between 0 and 1, where 0 corresponds with perfect equality (where everyone has the same income) and 1 corresponds with perfect inequality (where one person has all the income, and everyone else has zero income).

Economic Disparity is a global phenomenon but certain countries including India have much greater disparities than others and are also more severely affected with serious consequences on the social fabric.

#### Global Economic Disparity

The global economic disparity can be judged by the following facts:

1.  See the following chart, which depicts the distribution of total liquid net worth in the world per person in 2012:

![](https://javedjamilhome.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/70830-06anohew-mwi_hikd.jpg?w=736)  

The chart shows that the top .001% (91 thousand people) hold 30 pc of the wealth, the next .01% (800 thousand people) 19 pc and the next .1% hold 30 pc, which means that .111 pc of the people hold 81 pc of the wealth. Bottom 99.9 pc hold barely 19 pc.

**2.** According to a study entitled “Divided we Stand: Why Inequality Keeps Rising” by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD)

*   “Income inequality in OECD countries is at its highest level for the past half century. The average income of the richest 10% of the population is about nine times that of the poorest 10% across the OECD, up from seven times 25 years ago.”
*   In the United States inequality has increased further from already high levels.
*   “Other traditionally more egalitarian countries, such as Germany, Denmark and Sweden, have seen the gap between rich and poor expand from 5 to 1 in the 1980s, to 6 to 1 today.”

**3.** A study by the World Institute for Development Economics Research at [United Nations University](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_University "United Nations University") reports that the richest 1% of adults alone owned 40% of global assets in the year 2000. The [_three_ richest people](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_billionaires_%282009%29 "List of billionaires (2009)") possess more [financial](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finance "Finance") assets than the lowest 48 nations combined. The combined wealth of the “10 million dollar millionaires” grew to nearly $41 trillion in 2008.

**4.** Over the two decades prior to the onset of the global financial crisis, real disposable household incomes increased an average of 1.7% a year in its 34 member countries. However, the gap between rich and poor widened in most nations — the OECD journalist resource (2011–05) entitled “Growing Income Inequality in OECD Countries” states that with the exceptions of only France, Japan and Spain, wages of the 10% best-paid workers have risen relative to those of the 10% least-paid workers and the differential between the top and bottom 10% varies greatly from country to country: “While this ratio is much lower in the Nordic countries and in many continental European countries, it rises to around 14 to 1 in Israel, Turkey and the United States, to a high of 27 to 1 in Chile and Mexico.”
